Should You Give a Recruiter Your Social Security Number?

Employers may eventually need your Social Security number, but an early or unexpected request can be a serious job-scam warning sign.

Written and reviewed by Nehal Patel · Published August 5, 2026 · Approximately 7 minutes

The quick answer

Do not give an unverified recruiter your Social Security number. A legitimate employer may need it after you have been hired for employment, payroll, and tax records. It should not be the focus of an unsolicited message, initial interview, or rushed application process.

When an SSN request becomes suspicious

Job scammers often impersonate real companies because a familiar name can make a request for personal information seem routine. They may copy a real job description, recruiter name, company logo, email signature, or employee profile.

The Federal Trade Commission warns that fake employers may request a Social Security number, driver’s license, or banking information while pretending to complete employment paperwork. A request is especially concerning when it happens before a meaningful interview or before the employer has explained the position.

Stop if the request is unexpected or rushed

Do not provide your SSN merely because someone says it is required to schedule an interview, release a job description, verify your age, reserve a position, or begin receiving assignments.

When a legitimate employer may need your SSN

A real employer generally needs identifying information after hiring so it can maintain employment and tax records. The IRS lists an employee’s Social Security number among the information an employer needs when hiring employees.

That does not mean every person claiming to be an employer is entitled to the information. Before submitting it, you should have independently verified the company, confirmed the job, completed a credible hiring process, and received clear onboarding instructions.

Timing and method matter

A request during verified formal onboarding is different from a request in an unsolicited text message. Ask why the information is required, who will receive it, and whether it can be submitted through the company’s official secure employee system.

Seven checks before sharing sensitive information

1. Confirm that the company and job exist

Find the company’s website yourself instead of using the recruiter’s link. Look for the position on the official careers page. If it is not listed, contact the company and ask whether the opening is real.

2. Verify the recruiter independently

Contact the employer using a telephone number or email address found on its official website. Do not use contact information supplied by the recruiter to verify that same recruiter.

3. Inspect the email domain

Expand the sender details and compare everything after the @ symbol with the company’s real domain. Look for extra words, hyphens, misspellings, repeated letters, and personal email services.

4. Evaluate the interview

A credible interview should include meaningful discussion of your qualifications and the position. Immediate hiring through a text-only conversation requires additional verification.

5. Ask why the SSN is needed now

Request a clear explanation. Be suspicious if the recruiter claims the number is needed merely to provide job details, schedule an interview, verify your age, or guarantee the position.

6. Ask for a secure submission method

Avoid sending an SSN through ordinary email, text messages, or messaging apps. After verifying the employer, ask whether sensitive onboarding information can be entered into its official secure system.

7. Pause when you feel pressured

A legitimate employer should allow reasonable questions about the handling of sensitive information. Urgency is not a substitute for verification.

What information should you protect?

An SSN is not the only information a fake recruiter may seek. Treat the following as sensitive and verify the employer before sharing it:

  • Social Security number or photograph of the card
  • Driver’s license, passport, or other identification
  • Bank-account and routing numbers
  • Credit-card information
  • Passwords, security answers, or login credentials
  • One-time verification or authentication codes
  • Date of birth combined with other identity information
  • Photographs or scans of completed employment forms

Never give a recruiter a password or one-time verification code. Those items are not normal employment paperwork.

What if you already shared your SSN?

Sharing an SSN does not guarantee that identity theft will occur, but it deserves immediate attention—especially if the employer cannot be verified.

  1. Stop communicating with the suspected scammer.
  2. Preserve the evidence, including messages, email addresses, telephone numbers, websites, forms, and screenshots.
  3. Visit IdentityTheft.gov to report what happened and create a recovery plan.
  4. Follow the Social Security Administration’s guidance for an exposed or stolen Social Security number.
  5. Review your credit reports and consider placing a credit freeze with each nationwide credit bureau.
  6. Change any passwords you shared or reused and enable multifactor authentication where available.
  7. Report the fake job or recruiter to the platform where you found it.

Frequently asked questions

Should I give a recruiter my Social Security number?

Not during an unsolicited conversation or before independently verifying the employer and job. A verified employer may need it during formal onboarding after hiring.

Can a company ask for my SSN on a job application?

Practices vary, but you should understand why the information is requested and verify the employer before providing it. An early SSN request is a reason to pause, especially when the contact was unsolicited or the hiring process is unclear.

Is a secure-looking form proof that the employer is legitimate?

No. Scammers can create professional-looking forms and websites. Verify the company, recruiter, domain, and position independently before entering sensitive information.

Is it safe to send an SSN through email?

Ordinary email is a poor place for highly sensitive information. Verify the employer and ask whether the information can be submitted through an official secure onboarding system.

What if the recruiter says the request is urgent?

Do not let urgency replace verification. Pause and contact the company through independently located contact information.
